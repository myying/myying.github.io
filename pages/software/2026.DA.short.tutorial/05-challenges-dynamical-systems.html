So far the analysis has been a one-shot, Gaussian problem. Real models are chaotic: forecasts spread out over time, and errors stop looking Gaussian. This chapter shows why, with two toy models: Lorenz-96 for chaos and predictability, and vort3d, a simplified hurricane model, for non-Gaussian error.

1 · Chaos: the forecast spreads

Lorenz-96 is 40 variables on a ring, chaotic and deterministic: two states that start almost identical separate exponentially over time. A weather model does the same with its own initial-condition errors — which is why data assimilation exists: to shrink those errors before they have time to grow.

Try it: slide the initial perturbation $\sigma$ down and the fan in panel (a) tightens while the growth curves in panel (b) shift right; slide it up and the ensemble starts already-spread.

Panel (b) shows the whole story: error grows exponentially at first, then saturates once it reaches the model's natural variability — beyond that point, the forecast is no better than a random guess. Notice that spread tracks error closely: the ensemble's confidence is honest here, because it was built to match exactly this kind of error.

2 · A real process: rapid intensification in the vort3d hurricane model

The demo below launches 20 forecasts of the same simulated hurricane from slightly different starting states. Two things stand out that a Gaussian picture can't capture:

Rapid intensification is a regime change. Some members intensify quickly, others stall — the intensity panel splits into distinct stories, not a spread around one number.

Position error is non-Gaussian. Members that agree on storm strength can disagree wildly on where it is — so at a fixed point, the error distribution is bimodal and skewed, not a bell curve. That's what motivates the nonlinear filters in Chapter 6.
