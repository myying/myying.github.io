Data assimilation combines a forecast with an observation to get the analysis — the best estimate, with its own uncertainty. For Gaussian inputs, Bayes' rule gives a simple closed form. This page shows that rule in one variable, then two.

1-D: one variable — the analysis as a weighted average

One variable, one observation. The forecast is a Gaussian $N(\mu_b, \sigma_b^2)$; the observation is $N(y, \sigma_o^2)$. The analysis is their weighted average, weighted by certainty — the more precise input pulls harder.

Try it: drag the sliders to set the prior (mean and std) and the observation likelihood (mean and std) — the posterior updates live, always sitting between its two inputs and pulled toward the more certain one.

Reading the panels — 1-D

Prior (blue) — the forecast, centred at $\mu_b$ with spread $\sigma_b$. Wider means less certain. Observation (amber) — centred on $y$, with spread $\sigma_o$ set by instrument error. Posterior (red) — the analysis: always narrower than both inputs, and pulled toward whichever one is more precise.

The dashed curve, when the toggle is on, is the raw product of the two curves before rescaling — the posterior is just that product, normalized to integrate to one. That's the whole idea; every DA method (Kalman filter, EnKF, 3D-Var) computes this same thing, just at higher dimension.

2-D: two variables, one observed

Real states have many variables, linked by covariance — how error in one relates to error in another. Here, only $y$ is observed, but if $x$ and $y$ are correlated, observing $y$ sharpens $x$ too. The main panel shows the joint distribution as ellipses; the side panels show each variable on its own.

Try it: drag the correlation slider ρ to zero — the observation of y stops affecting x; raise it, and information leaks from the observed variable into the unobserved one. Watch the marginals and the ellipses tilt and shrink.

Reading the panels — 2-D

The ellipses are the joint distribution at 1, 2, 3σ; their tilt is the correlation $\rho$. The amber band is the observation of $y$ only. The red ellipses are the posterior: with $\rho = 0.6$, they shrink a lot in $y$ and noticeably in $x$ too — that's information leaking across the correlation.

The side panels show each variable's own distribution before (blue) and after (red). The key point: $x$'s distribution sharpens even though $x$ was never observed. Drag $\rho$ to 0 and that effect disappears — the blue and red $x$-curves become identical.
