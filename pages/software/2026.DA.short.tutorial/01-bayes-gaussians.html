Data assimilation combines a forecast with an observation to get the analysis — the best estimate, with its own uncertainty. Treat the true state $x$ as a random variable: before the observation, our belief about it is the prior $p(x)$, i.e. the forecast pdf. The observation $y$ carries information about $x$ through the likelihood $p(y \mid x)$ — how probable that observation would be for each possible $x$. Bayes' rule combines the two into the posterior, our updated belief after seeing $y$:

$$p(x \mid y) \;=\; \dfrac{p(y \mid x)\, p(x)}{p(y)} \;\propto\; p(y \mid x)\, p(x)$$

$p(y)$ is just a normalizing constant — it doesn't depend on $x$ — so the posterior is proportional to prior × likelihood; normalizing it to integrate to one gives the analysis pdf. This holds for any distributions. The special property of Gaussians is that this product is itself Gaussian, with a closed-form mean and variance — no numerical integration needed. That's what makes Bayes' rule practical for DA at scale: Kalman filters, ensemble Kalman filters, and 3D/4D-Var all lean on this Gaussian closure in one form or another. This page shows that closed form in one variable, then two.

1-D: one variable — the analysis as a weighted average

One variable, one observation. The prior is $p(x) = N(\mu_b, \sigma_b^2)$; the likelihood, as a function of $x$ for the fixed observed value $y$, is $p(y \mid x) = N(x; y, \sigma_o^2)$ — i.e. observation error is Gaussian, so this looks like a Gaussian centred at $y$ when plotted against $x$. Multiplying the two Gaussians and normalizing (Bayes' rule above) gives another Gaussian, the posterior $p(x \mid y) = N(\mu_a, \sigma_a^2)$, with

$$\mu_a = \mu_b + \dfrac{\sigma_b^2}{\sigma_b^2+\sigma_o^2}(y-\mu_b), \qquad \dfrac{1}{\sigma_a^2} = \dfrac{1}{\sigma_b^2} + \dfrac{1}{\sigma_o^2}$$

The mean $\mu_a$ is a precision-weighted average of $\mu_b$ and $y$ — the more precise input (smaller $\sigma$) pulls harder. The posterior variance $\sigma_a^2$ combines the two precisions ($1/\sigma^2$) additively, which is why $\sigma_a$ is always smaller than either $\sigma_b$ or $\sigma_o$: combining two independent pieces of information can only sharpen the estimate.

Try it: drag the sliders to set the prior (mean and std) and the observation likelihood (mean and std) — the posterior updates live, always sitting between its two inputs and pulled toward the more certain one.

2-D: two variables, one observed

Real states have many variables, linked by covariance — how error in one relates to error in another. Here, only $y$ is observed, but if $x$ and $y$ are correlated, observing $y$ sharpens $x$ too. The main panel shows the joint distribution as ellipses; the side panels show each variable on its own.

Try it: drag the correlation slider ρ to zero — the observation of y stops affecting x; raise it, and information leaks from the observed variable into the unobserved one. Watch the marginals and the ellipses tilt and shrink.
