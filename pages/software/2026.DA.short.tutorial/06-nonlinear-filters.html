Nonlinear filters

Every filter so far assumed a Gaussian prior. That breaks down for coherent structures like storms, where uncertainty is mostly about position. This chapter shows why, then builds up to particle filters, which drop the Gaussian assumption entirely.

1 · Nonlinearity breaks Gaussian assumption

Every filter so far leaned on one fact: a Gaussian pushed through a linear map is still Gaussian. That's why Chapter 1's product-of-Gaussians trick worked exactly. Push a Gaussian through a nonlinear map $y = f(x)$ instead, and $y$ is generally no longer Gaussian — it can pile up unevenly on one side (skewed) or grow heavier or lighter tails (over- or under-kurtotic) than a bell curve with the same mean and variance.

Position error is a clean example of exactly this. A warm blob's centre $x$ is Gaussian-distributed (spread Lsprd controls how much) — but the quantity a thermometer at a fixed point P actually measures is $y = f(x) = T(\lvert x - x_P\rvert)$, the blob's temperature profile evaluated at P's distance from the centre. That's a non-monotone, nonlinear function of $x$ (it peaks when the centre lands on P and falls off both ways), so even though $x$ is perfectly Gaussian, $y$ is not. Panel (b)'s readout tracks skew and kurtosis — near zero and near 3 respectively for a Gaussian — as a quick check of how far $y$ has drifted from Gaussian at the current spread.

Why the bend. The value at P peaks when a member's centre lands exactly on P, and falls off on both sides — a non-monotone relationship. Push a Gaussian cloud of centres through that, and the temperature at P comes out skewed, not Gaussian, even though the position uncertainty itself was perfectly Gaussian.

2 · EnKF vs particle filter

The EnKF's update, $x_a^{(m)} = x_b^{(m)} + K(y - Hx_b^{(m)})$, only ever shifts each member by the same gain $K$ — it can reshape a Gaussian, but not turn it into something skewed. The particle filter takes a different approach: leave every prior member $x_b^{(m)}$ where it is, and instead attach it an importance weight — how consistent that member is with the observation, from the observation likelihood:

$$\tilde w_m = p\big(y \mid x_b^{(m)}\big) = \exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\big(y - Hx_b^{(m)}\big)^2}{2\sigma_o^2}\right), \qquad w_m = \dfrac{\tilde w_m}{\sum_{m'=1}^{N} \tilde w_{m'}}$$

Normalizing so the weights sum to one turns them into a discrete posterior: the analysis is the weighted particle cloud $\{x_b^{(m)}, w_m\}$ itself, with weighted mean $\bar x_a = \sum_m w_m\, x_b^{(m)}$. Since no Gaussian shape is assumed anywhere in this step, the weights can land however the likelihood says — including on a skewed prior like section 1's, which is exactly where the EnKF's shift-only update struggles.

The catch is weight collapse: if only a few members sit near the observation, those few get almost all the weight and the rest become deadweight. The standard diagnostic is the effective sample size,

$$N_{\text{eff}} = \dfrac{1}{\sum_{m=1}^{N} w_m^2}$$

which ranges from 1 (one particle has all the weight) to $N$ (all weights equal). Small $N_{\text{eff}}$ relative to $N$ means most particles are contributing nothing — the classic failure mode of particle filters in high dimensions.
