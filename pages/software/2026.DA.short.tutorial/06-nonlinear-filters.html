Nonlinear filters

Every filter so far assumed a Gaussian prior. That breaks down for coherent structures like storms, where uncertainty is mostly about position. This chapter shows why, then builds up to particle filters, which drop the Gaussian assumption entirely.

1 · Position errors break the Gaussian assumption

A warm blob whose members only differ in where it's centred — spread Lsprd controls how much. We watch the temperature at a fixed point P. Click P to move it, or a dot in panel (c) to highlight that member. At small spread, the value at P is roughly Gaussian; once the spread is large enough that members' centres sweep past P, the distribution skews hard and stops looking Gaussian at all.

Why the bend. The value at P peaks when a member's centre lands exactly on P, and falls off on both sides — a non-monotone relationship. Push a Gaussian cloud of centres through that, and the temperature at P comes out skewed, not Gaussian, even though the position uncertainty itself was perfectly Gaussian.

2 · EnKF vs particle filter

The particle filter reweights each prior member by how well it matches the observation, instead of shifting members like the EnKF does. Since it never assumes a Gaussian shape, it can handle the skewed prior from section 1. The three panels below show the same ensemble before and after assimilating one observation at P: the prior, the EnKF analysis, and the particle-filter posterior (members light up by their weight).

Try it: slide Lsprd up — the EnKF only tightens where a linear update points, while the particle filter lights up just the members actually consistent with the observation. Watch Neff in the readout: it collapses as the observation becomes unlikely under the prior — a sign of too few effective particles.

Reading the panels. (a) The prior: a wide band of rings, the possible positions. (b) The EnKF: the same update as Chapter 3, applied per grid cell — it can only tighten the band, not slide it, so it distorts instead of shifting. (c) The particle filter: the same members, but only the ones consistent with the observation light up — a direct picture of how few particles actually matter, which Neff in the readout quantifies.
